An Agent That Audits Kaspar-HFT System for best Design Principles

An AI agent that measures a running Kaspar-HFT system against the design principles, reports which principles the system satisfies, and proposes the architecture changes the measurements justify, each with its predicted and trial-measured effect.

Vincent Mayeski, M2 Tech, Montreal

1. Introduction

The preceding article derived four design principles for market-data receivers from over one year of CME packet data:

  1. A receiver whose per-packet service time is below the publisher period, the minimum interval between consecutive packets from the exchange, never queues; one thread is optimal.
  2. Above the publisher period, clustered arrivals build a queueing tail, and a chain partitioned into stages on separate cores has lower tail latency than one thread.
  3. Only the slowest stage determines end-to-end latency; a partition that does not shorten it costs a hop and gains nothing.
  4. Just below the period, the residual tail comes from packets of many messages decoded sequentially; the remedy is per-message cost, not threads.

The principles are stated in terms of measured quantities: the publisher period of the feed, the per-packet service time of each stage under load, the cost of a thread hop, and the number of messages per packet. These are properties of the feed and of the deployment, not of the source code, so whether a deployed system satisfies the principles is established by measurement on the assembled system against its feed.

The procedure is fixed: measure the publisher period, the hop cost and the service time of each stage; compare each against the principles; where a principle is violated, propose the change that the measurements justify and measure its effect on a recorded tape; report. This article describes an agent that executes it: the quantities it observes, the criteria it applies, the proposals it produces. The article presents a design. The agent does not exist, and Section 5 states what Kaspar-HFT lacks to support it.

2. The criteria

The design flow of the measurement study, expressed as criteria that a running system satisfies or violates, reduces to the seven of Figure 1.

Two of the criteria require elaboration.

Criterion 1 requires that the publisher period be measured on the target feed. On NQ the period is approximately 7.5 μs and was invariant between quiet and volatile sessions; this is a property of one feed's publisher and is not assumed to hold elsewhere. The agent obtains the period from the first percentile of the distribution of gaps between consecutive packets. The mean gap is approximately three hundred times the period and is not used.

Criterion 2 compares the service time of a stage with the publisher period using the upper quantiles of service under load. A stage whose median service time is below the period and whose 99th percentile exceeds it accumulates a queue on the packets that determine the latency tail.

3. The latency probe, and how the agent uses it

Kaspar-HFT carries a latency probe. It is a passive actor that subscribes to an order book and, for every update the book publishes, reads two timestamps the pipeline has already placed on the update and takes a third of its own. The first is taken by the socket reader when the read call for the packet returns. The second is taken by the order book when it publishes the update. The third is taken by the probe on entry to its handler. The difference between the first two is the first leg, socket read to book publication; the difference between the last two is the second leg, publication to subscriber. The probe also reads from the update the depth of the first mailbox at the moment the packet was enqueued, and the position of the message within its packet. It writes two records: one per published message, with the publication time, the first leg, the mailbox depth and the index within the packet; and one per packet, with the read time, the sequence number and the number of messages. The probe sends nothing back and modifies no book state. It is compiled in with the order-by-order book and enabled by a configuration key; disabled, it has no subscriber and costs nothing per message. Figure 2 shows the path it measures.

The probe measures the market-data path only, from the socket read to the book and on to a subscriber. It does not measure the order path, and it observes nothing before the socket read.

The agent works as follows. It builds the system with the probe and runs it on the live feed, or on a recorded tape in the simulator. It reads the probe's two records and computes from them the quantities of Section 4: the publisher period, the hop cost, the service floor of the chain, the cost per message, and the end-to-end quantiles. It compares each against the criteria of Section 2 and records a verdict. For each violation it forms a proposal, applies the proposal in a separate build, runs the same tape, and records the measured effect. It writes a report of the verdicts and the proposals. The service time of each stage, which the probe does not supply, requires the per-stage timing of Section 5.

4. Measurements

The agent makes every measurement the criteria require. Figure 3 lists them with the method for each. The methods for the publisher period, the hop cost and the service floor are those of the measurement study; the per-stage timing and the per-boundary hop cost require items 2 and 3 of Section 5.

Publisher period. From the per-packet record, the agent forms the distribution of gaps between consecutive read timestamps within each 30-minute window and takes its first percentile. The result is reported as the median over windows with its range. The agent also computes the first-percentile gap within each quintile of packet rate; on NQ the measurement study found it constant at 7.43 to 7.53 μs while the rate varied by a factor of four, and the agent reports whether the same holds on the feed under test. The mean gap is reported separately and is not used in any criterion.

Hop cost. The one-way cost of an asynchronous boundary is obtained in three ways and all are reported. The first is the framework's own benchmark: two actors on two threads exchange a message and its reply through the mailbox type in use, and the hop cost is half the median round trip. On the reference host this gives approximately 3.4 μs round trip through the default mailbox, so 1.7 μs one way; 90 ns for two actors in a group on one thread; and 30 ns for a synchronous call. The second is in situ: the measurement study found that the end-to-end median is the service time plus one hop cost per asynchronous boundary in almost every cell of its sweep, so the shift in the end-to-end median between two probe runs that differ in one boundary's delivery mode is a direct measurement of that boundary's hop cost on the live path. The third is per message: with the enqueue timestamp of Section 5, item 3, in the message header, the wait in each mailbox is read directly at dequeue, which gives the hop cost per boundary rather than for the chain. Disagreement among the three beyond a few hundred nanoseconds is reported.

Service floor of the chain. The time from socket read to book publication for a message that encountered no queueing is obtained by the three estimators of the measurement study, which the agent requires to agree. The first is the median first leg over messages that were enqueued when the first mailbox was empty and were first in their packet; for these neither queueing nor the decode of preceding messages in the same packet contributes. The second is the intercept of the median ladder of first leg against index within the packet. The third is the median first leg of one-message packets inside a burst, identified from the per-packet record as packets spaced one publisher period apart. On the two-stage receiver of the measurement study the three estimators gave 7.0 to 7.5 μs on each of three instruments; on the single-threaded receiver the floor is below 1 μs.

Cost per message. The slope of the median ladder of first leg against index within the packet is the time to decode and apply one additional message. The same slope is obtained from first leg against span, and the two are compared. The cost per message, with the service floor, gives the service time of a packet of any length, which criterion 2 compares with the publisher period at the upper quantiles of span.

Service time of each stage. The probe takes no timestamp between the socket read and the book publication, so the service of an individual stage is not available from the probe. The agent obtains it from an instrumented build run in the simulator on a recorded tape, in which a timing wrapper records the duration of each handler invocation per message. The actors, the messages and the handlers are those of production; the simulator supplies the packets with their recorded timestamps. From this the agent has, for arbitration, decode, book and strategy separately, the median and upper quantiles of service by span. The sum over stages is checked against the chain floor measured live, and the difference is attributed to the hand-offs and compared with the in-situ hop cost. This instrumentation does not exist (Section 5, item 2).

Queueing component. From the per-message record, the first leg against the mailbox depth at enqueue gives, by regression, the time a message waits per message ahead of it, which is the service time of the stage that drains the mailbox. This is a second estimate of that stage's service time, taken on the live path.

End-to-end latency. The median, 99th and 99.9th percentiles of the first leg over all published messages, for the book stream and the trade stream separately, with warm-up and recovery windows excluded. This is the quantity compared between the baseline and each trialled proposal.

Span distribution. The quantiles of messages per packet, and the share of packets, of messages and of decode time held by packets of ten or more messages, where decode time is span multiplied by the cost per message. This is the measurement behind the finding of criterion 4.

Effect of spinning and pinning. Where the inventory records a spinning mailbox type or a pinned busy-polling thread, the agent runs the probe with and without it on the same tape and reports the median, 99th percentile and maximum of each, together with the core occupancy of the spinning thread from process statistics.

5. What is missing in Kaspar-HFT

The probe and its records, the mailbox-depth field in the message header, the analysis scripts, the mailbox types, actor groups, the two delivery modes and deterministic replay exist. Figure 4 lists what does not.

  1. A production build with no instrumentation. The probe's timestamps are taken unconditionally in the live build. One compile-time define must remove all measurement code from production, so that the production median excludes its cost.
  2. Per-stage timing. The framework has no hook that times a handler. The duration of each handler invocation, under the define, gives the service time of each stage.
  3. Mailbox wait per message. The message header carries the mailbox depth at enqueue but not the time. An enqueue timestamp, read at dequeue, gives the hop cost per boundary in situ.
  4. Delivery mode set by configuration. The mode is chosen in code at each call site. A table read at start-up lets a trial change a boundary without a code edit.
  5. A topology listing. Actor, thread, group, mailbox type and pinning, in one place.
  6. Paced replay. Replay is deterministic and unpaced, so mailbox depth in replay does not reproduce live arrivals. A mode that releases packets at their recorded times is needed for trials.
  7. The skill. The procedure, the criteria, the measurement recipes and the report template, in the repository.

None of these changes a handler, a message type or the order book.

6. Procedure

Inventory. From source and configuration: the actors on the market-data path in order, the delivery mode at each boundary, group membership, mailbox type, pinning, and which stages keep state between packets.

Probe run. Build with the probe, run a window on the live feed or a tape, exclude the warm-up period and recovery windows, compute percentiles from the per-message record.

Measurement. The quantities of Section 4.

Comparison and verdict. Each stage's 99th-percentile service against the publisher period (Figure 6); each partition against criterion 3; each boundary's delivery mode against criterion 5; the order of stage sizes against criterion 6; spinning and pinning against criterion 7.

Proposal. One topology change per violation, with the criterion it addresses and the effect predicted from the design rule and the measured service times. The forms a proposal can take are listed in Section 7.

Trial. The proposal applied in a separate build and run on the same tape; median and p99 against the baseline. One proposal per trial, since effects are not additive. Production is not modified. Proposals that require a code change are reported without a trial.

Report. Per criterion: the measured value, the threshold and the verdict. Per proposal: the change, the predicted effect, the trial result and a recommendation, ranked by measured improvement under the operator's weighting of median and tail. Applying a proposal is the operator's decision. Figure 7 shows the form.

7. Proposals

A proposal is one of the following, each tied to the criterion whose measurement motivates it.

  • Change the delivery mode of a boundary between synchronous and asynchronous (criterion 5). In Kaspar-HFT this is a one-line change at the sender's call site and the receiving handler is unaffected.
  • Partition a stage whose upper-quantile service exceeds the publisher period, at the natural boundary that most nearly halves it, with the larger part at ingress (criteria 2, 6). The proposal names the boundary and the predicted service of each part.
  • Pool a stateless stage above the period across several cores instead of partitioning it (criterion 6), where the inventory shows the stage carries no state between packets.
  • Remove a partition whose removal does not increase the largest stage (criterion 3), since the hop it costs reduces no queueing.
  • Change an actor's group membership or mailbox type, or remove a spinning mailbox or a pinned thread for which the with-and-without comparison shows no tail improvement (criterion 7).
  • Reduce the cost per message in a stage, or move the stage to the FPGA handler (criterion 4), where the measurement shows the tail is carried by long packets. These are code changes and are reported without a trial.

The first five are deployment parameters: a configuration value or a single-line change at a call site, none of which affects a handler, a message type or a test, so a trial in the simulator exercises the same code as production. The agent trials them and reports the result. It applies none of them to the production system.

The sixth is outside the deployment parameters. Criterion 4 states that for a chain below the publisher period, the residual tail arises from packets containing many messages, decoded sequentially, and from service-time variance. The agent measures both: the slope per message, and the share of packets, messages and decode time held by long packets. On the NQ book stream the measurement study found that packets of ten or more messages constitute under 2% of packets and several times that share of messages; on ZN trade, 11% of packets contain 66% of the messages and consume 86% of the decode time. A finding of this kind is not addressed by a deployment parameter. The agent reports it as a proposal with the two available interventions: reduction of the decoder's cost per message, or migration of the decode to the FPGA handler, where the work remaining after a packet's last byte is independent of packet length. The measurement that motivates the proposal, the share of decode time in long packets, is attached.

Proposals under criterion 7 are likewise reported with their trial result. A spinning mailbox or a pinned busy-polling thread may be justified. The measurement study predicts that on a self-exciting feed the receiver is already active and cache-warm at the arrival of each tail-determining message, so that the intervention affects the median and the minimum and not the tail, at a steady cost in core occupancy and cache pressure. The agent measures the three quantiles with and without the intervention on the same tape and reports them with the cost, and recommends removal only where the tail is unchanged.

8. Invocation

The agent runs within Claude Code in a copy of the Kaspar-HFT repository, on a host that receives the feed or holds a tape. A request names the handler and the window:

Audit the NQ book handler against the design principles on a 15-minute live window at the open.
Audit the ES and ZN handlers on yesterday's tape and report whether any boundary should change.

The agent produces the inventory, runs the probe, applies the criteria, trials each proposal on the tape, and writes the report, the proposals and the per-message records to a dedicated directory. Each proposal is accompanied by the diff of one line or one configuration key that would apply it, so that the operator can apply it directly or discard it. No exchange credential is required; a live window requires only the multicast feed the host already receives.

9. Limitations

The probe measures from the socket read, so the NIC, the kernel or Onload receive path, and the wire are outside the agent's observation; a violation upstream of the socket read is not detected. Proposals are trialled one at a time, so the report does not establish the effect of applying several together; an operator who applies one proposal re-runs the agent before considering the next. The criteria are those of a self-exciting feed with a bounded publisher rate; a feed with a different arrival law requires the measurement study to be repeated before the criteria can be stated. The skill itself is a design. The probe, its analysis scripts, the per-boundary delivery modes and the mailbox types are present in the repository and in use; the items of Section 5 and the agent itself are not implemented. Until item 1 is made, the live build carries the probe's timestamps unconditionally, and the median it reports includes their cost. The probe takes no timestamp between the socket read and the book publication, so the first leg is not subdivided; nothing before the socket read is observed; and all counts are over updates the book published, not over packets received.

10. Conclusion

The design principles of the measurement study are stated in terms of measured quantities, and whether a system satisfies them on its feed is determined by measurement. The determination is a procedure: record the topology, run the probe under valid conditions, measure the period and the service times, compare against the criteria, form the proposals the comparison justifies, trial each on the tape, and report. Kaspar-HFT provides the instruments and makes each topology decision a deployment parameter that can be changed without modifying code. The audit agent executes the procedure, reports which criteria the system satisfies, proposes the changes the measurements justify with the predicted and trial-measured effect of each, and leaves the decision to apply them to the operator.

Kaspar-HFT is open source: github.com/vincent212/kaspar-hft. The measurement tooling is under kaspr/perf; the design principles are in arXiv:2609.32848.
