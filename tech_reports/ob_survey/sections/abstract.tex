\begin{abstract}
The limit order book (LOB) is the principal observable representation of liquidity in electronic
markets. A large literature extracts predictive information from it: order-flow imbalance, queue
imbalance and the micro-price; stochastic queue and first-passage models; Hawkes and neural point
processes; and convolutional, recurrent, attention-based and spatial-temporal Transformer models,
pretrained message-level encoders, generative models and hybrids that combine statistical structure
with learned representations. A substantial part of that literature evaluates models primarily as statistical forecasters, and large
independent re-tests find that many published deep models lose much of their accuracy on new data. This
survey asks a different question: \emph{how does information contained in the limit order book
become an executable trading decision, and how should prediction, queue dynamics, simulation and
execution be evaluated together?}

We organise the literature along four axes---representation, model class, prediction objective
and economic role---and follow a single chain throughout:
LOB state $\to$ representation $\to$ prediction $\to$ decision $\to$ queue $\to$ execution $\to$
P\&L. Each link can destroy information or add uncertainty. We distinguish models that predict
prices or events from models that estimate execution outcomes---fill probability, queue depletion,
adverse selection---and argue that residual queue position and conditional execution value form
the missing translation layer between LOB prediction research and trading.

Two further parts are specific to this survey. First, we describe a deterministic, queue-exact
replay architecture (the Kaspar system for CME futures) in which simulated orders enter the
reconstructed historical first-in-first-out queue and are filled only by recorded executions that
reach an order behind them; the same strategy and order-management code runs in replay and live
modes. Second, we describe LLM-Assisted Model Discovery (\lamd), in which a language model proposes
candidate models while fitting, replay on observed market data, validation and statistical
certification remain external to the language model and reproducible under fixed inputs and
procedures. We take the position that the market is observed, not generated: within the methodology
proposed here, language models generate hypotheses and candidate models, not the market data used as
evidence.

Two criteria organise the evaluation: \emph{tradeability}, under which predictive performance is
judged after latency, spread, fees, queue position and adverse selection; and \emph{Occam's
razor}, under which each additional layer of complexity---model capacity, hardware, and the
breadth of the search that produced the model---must earn incremental out-of-sample economic
value. This is a theoretical and methodological paper: all numerical examples are stylised, and no
new experiments are reported.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} limit order book, market microstructure, order-flow imbalance, micro-price,
Hawkes process, queue position, first-passage time, adverse selection, deterministic simulation, deep
learning, spatial-temporal Transformer, foundation models, uncertainty quantification, multiple testing,
deflated Sharpe ratio, Occam's razor, LLM-assisted model discovery.
\end{abstract}
