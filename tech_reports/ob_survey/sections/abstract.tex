\begin{abstract}
The limit order book (LOB) is the principal observable representation of liquidity in electronic
markets. A large literature extracts predictive information from it: order-flow imbalance, queue
imbalance and the micro-price; stochastic queue and first-passage models; Hawkes and neural point
processes; and convolutional, recurrent, attention-based and spatial-temporal Transformer models,
pretrained message-level encoders, generative models and hybrids that combine statistical structure
with learned representations. A substantial part of that literature evaluates models primarily as statistical
forecasters, and large independent re-tests find that many published deep models lose much of their accuracy on new
data. This
survey asks a different question: \emph{how does information contained in the limit order book
become an executable trading decision, and how should prediction, queue dynamics, simulation and
execution be evaluated together?} Its thesis is that a limit-order-book model is useful not because it predicts the
next price move but if the information it extracts survives representation, prediction, decision, queue position,
latency and execution to positive out-of-sample economic value; the quantity that matters for trading is the expected
profit of an action given the market state and the order's place in the queue.

The survey is three surveys in one, arranged in the order a model follows on its way to production. The first
covers the theory of order-book models: how each is built, what its properties are and what it predicts. The second
covers the simulators in which a model is turned into a trading strategy and tested. The third covers the hardware on
which a tested strategy is deployed. The three are rarely treated together, but a model has to pass through all of
them to reach production, and a practitioner or student who wants to take one there needs all three; that path is
the reason for the survey's structure.

The chain LOB state $\to$ representation $\to$ prediction $\to$ decision $\to$ queue $\to$ execution $\to$ P\&L
frames the survey: each link can destroy information or add uncertainty. We place the main works on four
axes---representation, model class, prediction objective and economic role---and distinguish models that predict prices or
events from models that estimate execution outcomes such as fill probability, queue depletion and adverse selection,
which we treat as modelling targets in their own right. For the learned models we explain, from first principles, how
they are built and trained, take apart meta-models and structural hybrids that combine them with classical models,
and show how much reported accuracy can come from the construction of the label and the data split rather than from
the network.

For simulation, we survey order-book simulators---historical replay, agent-based, model-based and generative---and
compare them on the properties execution questions need: per-order queue position, explicit latency, determinism and
a shared code path to live trading. \kasparhft{}\footnote{\kasparhft{} is available at \url{https://github.com/vincent212/kaspar-hft}.}, an open-source trading system and simulator, serves as the worked
example of a deterministic, queue-exact replay in which simulated orders enter the reconstructed historical
first-in-first-out queue and are filled only by recorded executions that reach an order behind them; we show how an
order-book model is added to it as an actor that publishes its predictions to a strategy. A strategy tested in its
simulator can be deployed as it is: as far as the code is concerned there is no difference between the simulated and
the deployed environment, and moving from one to the other is a configuration setting.
For deployment, we explain why order-book models are slow at inference---book building, sequential recurrence,
attention whose cost grows with the square of the sequence length, and memory-bound evaluation at batch
size one---why a model too slow for the hot path chokes the system, and which stages can be accelerated on FPGAs,
GPUs and other processors.

Two criteria run through the survey: \emph{tradeability}, under which predictive performance is
judged after latency, spread, fees, queue position and adverse selection; and \emph{Occam's
razor}, under which each additional layer of complexity---model capacity and hardware---must earn
incremental out-of-sample economic value. This is a survey and a methodological paper. Its numerical examples are
stylised, and its only computations are illustrations on synthetic data; it reports no new results on market data.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} limit order book, market microstructure, order-flow imbalance, micro-price,
Hawkes process, queue position, first-passage time, adverse selection, deterministic simulation, deep
learning, spatial-temporal Transformer, foundation models, uncertainty quantification, multiple testing,
Occam's razor, market simulation, hardware acceleration, FPGA.
\end{abstract}
