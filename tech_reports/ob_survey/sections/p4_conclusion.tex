\subsection{Models}

The open problems in this group concern what the models of Part~II predict and how they are built.

\paragraph{Execution-aware objectives.} Most prediction models we reviewed output
an estimate of $\P(\Delta m_{t,H} > 0 \mid X_t)$. The more useful objective is an estimate of
$\E[\Pi \mid X_t, Q^{\text{ahead}}_t, a_t]$, the expected profit of each available action,
combining prediction, fill probability, queue position and adverse selection. Calibrated
confidence~\citep{manoharan2026uqlob} is a step toward it: it tells an execution policy when to act.

\paragraph{Message-level foundation models for execution.} Pretrained message-level encoders have
so far been evaluated mainly on mid-price and next-message prediction~\citep{linna2025lobert}.
Fine-tuning them for fill probability and conditional mark-outs, evaluated in queue-exact replay,
is an open direction.

\paragraph{State-space and long-context models for event streams.} State-space sequence models are well
suited to long, irregular event streams (Section~\ref{sec:deep-ssm}), yet apart from one hardware-oriented design~\citep{popov2026ssm} we found no established LOB forecaster built on them.
Testing them against simple baselines on the same inputs---not against each other---is a clear
next step.

\paragraph{Near-criticality and learned models.} Estimated branching ratios of order flow are high,
from about $0.7$--$0.8$ to about one depending on method (Section~\ref{sec:hawkes-crit}). How deep sequence
models behave on near-critical, long-memory inputs, and whether Hawkes structure built into them
(Section~\ref{sec:hybrid}) improves robustness, is untested.

\paragraph{A parsimony audit.} For each widely cited deep LOB model, re-estimate its incremental
economic value and performance corrected for the number of configurations tried against the best low-capacity baseline on the same data and
fill model. Such a meta-study would directly test the central claim of this survey.

\subsection{Execution and simulation}

The open problems in this group concern how predictions become orders and how simulators measure the result
(Sections~\ref{sec:sim-needs} and~\ref{sec:simulators}).

\paragraph{Queue-aware benchmarks.} Queue position should be a first-class state variable.
Benchmarks should report performance conditional on queue rank, residual volume, spread and expected
depletion time, and should measure the size of the queue-rank effect on fill probability and on
conditional mark-outs across assets and regimes, rather than take a single model estimate as
given.

\paragraph{End-to-end signal and execution.} In the studies we reviewed, most models output a forecast
and leave execution to a separate rule. Models whose output is the decision itself---quote, keep, cancel, cross---and whose
training objective is execution value would close the gap that this survey describes. Reinforcement
learning is one route, if the policy is trained and judged in queue-exact replay of recorded markets, against the
model-free benchmarks in the same replay, as in Section~\ref{sec:worked-kaspar}.

\paragraph{Causal rather than predictive questions.} The questions that matter for execution are
causal: they ask what happens to the queue and the price \emph{if} an order is placed at a given price. Replay with a
no-impact assumption answers this only for small orders. Models of the market's response to one's own
orders, measured and validated on real executions, remain open.

\paragraph{Generative evaluation.} Generators should be evaluated by whether they reproduce the stylised facts of order
books (such as the clustering of events), by conditional statistics and market-impact responses, and by usefulness
for a downstream task, rather than by marginal similarity alone~\citep{lobbench2025}.

\paragraph{Cross-asset information after costs.} An open question is when cross-asset information remains
valuable after costs, latency, multiple testing and parameter instability.

\paragraph{Simulators against live fills.} We found no comparison of the simulators of Table~\ref{tab:simulators} on the
same orders. Running one strategy through several of them and through paper trading, and comparing fill rates and
post-fill mark-outs with the live fills, would measure how much each simulator's assumptions---estimated queue
position, simulated order flow, no market impact---cost in accuracy.

\paragraph{Impact measured on the real market.} Replay cannot measure the market's response to one's own orders. In this
survey's approach it is measured on the real market, from paper and live executions and their mark-outs. How to measure it
precisely from few executions, and how to carry the measured response back into replay, are open.

\subsection{Hardware}

The open problems in this group follow from Section~\ref{sec:hardware}.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=1pt]
\item Training objectives that penalise expected inference latency and resource use on a target
  device, so that models trade accuracy against deployability explicitly.
\item Which components of spatial-temporal LOB models survive mapping to programmable logic without
  losing their economic value.
\item Hawkes intensities in hardware: exponential kernels update in constant time and suit pipelines,
  and a power-law kernel can be approximated by a few exponentials, each with its own running
  sum~\citep{bochud2007}; how many are needed at the precision of fixed-point hardware, and how
  state-dependent kernels map to pipelines, are open.
\item Whether the statistical properties of a strategy developed on one hardware and latency profile
  survive deployment on another---the research--production gap of Section~\ref{sec:kaspar-determinism}, at the hardware layer.
\item Public, auditable benchmarks that cover book building and inference together: STAC-T1 audits a whole
  tick-to-trade path with simple logic, and STAC-ML audits inference alone, excluding data ingestion and features;
  neither evaluates a learned model on a book it has built (Section~\ref{sec:hw-audited}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ================================================================

This section restates the path from theory to production that the survey follows, places the survey between the two
literatures it draws on, attaches its findings to the chain from
order book to profit, states the conclusions and the evidence for them, follows one model through the whole method in
\kasparhft{}, gives the order of operations it recommends, and ends with reading paths through the survey and the evidence
status of its main claims.

\subsection{From theory to production}
\label{sec:theory-to-production}

The survey has followed the path a model takes to production. Part~II covered the theory: how the models of the book
are built, what their properties are and what they predict. Part~III covered simulation, where a model becomes a
strategy and is tested against recorded markets with queue position and latency, and deployment, where a tested
strategy runs on hardware fast enough for it. The three are surveyed together because a model has to pass through all
of them: a model that predicts well but has not been simulated has not been shown to trade, and a strategy that works
in simulation but is too slow for the hot path cannot be deployed. \kasparhft{} connects the last two stages: the
strategy code tested in its simulator is the code that is deployed. As far as the code is concerned there is no
difference between the simulated and the deployed environment; moving from replay to paper and live trading is a
configuration setting (Section~\ref{sec:kaspar-equivalence}).

\subsection{Two literatures that are rarely read together}
\label{sec:two-literatures}

The work reviewed in this survey comes from two literatures that are usually read separately. The first forecasts the
market. It builds signals and learned models from the book and evaluates them by how often they predict the direction
of the next price move: accuracy and the F1 score, with many of its deep-learning results reported on the public FI-2010 data set (Section~\ref{sec:deep-io-fi2010}). In the hierarchy of Section~\ref{sec:three-problems} it works at levels 2
and 3, about the market rather than about one's own order. The second models what happens to an order once it is
sent: its place in the queue and its chance of being filled (Section~\ref{sec:queue-models}), the adverse selection of
its fills (Section~\ref{sec:adverse}), and its impact on the price~\citep{kyle1985,almgren2001}. Its classical models
have few parameters, and they work at levels 4 and 5.

The question this survey asks sits between the two: does the information a forecasting model extracts survive the
queue, the fill and the latency of a real order? In our reading of the literature the two rarely meet. We found no
pretrained order-book model evaluated on fills or profit (Section~\ref{sec:foundation}); none of the reinforcement-learning
studies we reviewed, which do learn to act, reports a live or paper-trading test (Section~\ref{sec:policy}); and, in the studies we
reviewed, a forecast is usually turned into orders by a separate, hand-written rule. Joining the two needs a test bench in which a
forecast changes an order, the order waits in a real queue, and the result is measured in money. That is what the
simulators of Part~III, and \kasparhft{} in particular, provide.

\subsection{Conclusions and the evidence for them}
\label{sec:conclusions-list}

Seven conclusions follow from the evidence reviewed.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Representation matters.} With the network held fixed, the choice of input decided most of the result
  in one large comparison~\citep{lucchese2024}.
\item \textbf{The prediction target matters.} How the label is built can decide the reported accuracy.
\item \textbf{Queue position matters.} Whether a passive order fills, and how good the fill is, depends on its place
  in the queue.
\item \textbf{Execution matters.} Resting and crossing can cost similar amounts once adverse selection is counted.
\item \textbf{Simulation assumptions matter.} A fill rule that ignores the queue overstates results; a replay without
  market impact answers questions about small orders only.
\item \textbf{Latency matters.} Cost rose with delay. A slow model on the hot path misses signals,
  leaves orders to be picked off, and lets message queues grow; a model's evaluation time is part of its
  specification.
\item \textbf{Complexity must earn its cost.} A more complex model is justified only by incremental out-of-sample
  economic value.
\end{enumerate}

Order-flow imbalance, queue imbalance, Hawkes intensities and first-passage models give
interpretable low-dimensional descriptions of market dynamics. Deep and pretrained models give
representational capacity and, increasingly, calibrated confidence; most of them are learned analogues of
the classical signal models rather than of the mechanistic ones, the queue and Hawkes models that describe how the
book evolves and can be run forward with an order inserted (Section~\ref{sec:deep-adds}). Meta-models and
structural hybrids combine the two, either by feeding classical outputs to a learner or by building classical
structure inside a network. The accuracy reported for deep models can depend strongly on how the label and the data
split are built; in one re-test, the construction of the label alone moved DeepLOB's accuracy from 65.9\% to
54.6\%~\citep{lee2024predictability}. The conclusion is not that simple models are superior. It is that complexity is an
empirical hypothesis: a more complex model is justified when it delivers incremental out-of-sample
economic value that survives realistic execution, temporal validation, and the multiple-testing
burden of its own selection.

Two kinds of infrastructure make that standard testable. The first is the simulator, and \kasparhft{} is the survey's
instrument for it: a queue-exact replay of the exchange's own feed, deterministic, with separately configurable
market-data and order delays, running many days at once and the same code in replay, paper and live trading
(Section~\ref{sec:kaspar}). On a year of E-mini data it shows the conclusions above in numbers: resting and crossing
cost similar amounts, about $+0.095$ ticks per contract; post-fill mark-outs show adverse selection completed within a
second; and with the same delay applied to orders, cancels and market data, from 0 to 5\,ms, cost rose with delay
for both versions, and more steeply for the aggressive one, reaching $+0.150$ (passive) and $+0.296$
(aggressive) ticks per contract at 5\,ms (the latency runs use each version's own settings, so their zero-delay costs
differ from the matched pair)~\citep{maciejewski_shadow}. This survey evaluates trading results on replay of
the recorded market (Principle~1); generated markets---agent-based, model-based or generative---serve other purposes
and are not its focus. A replay keeps the real market; the
market's response to one's own orders is measured from paper and live executions (Section~\ref{sec:sim-survey}).
Exact queue position in the recorded market
needs order-level data and an order-level book; among the replay tools compared, hftbacktest (with order-level
data), lobsim and \kasparhft{} provide it without altering the recorded market (Section~\ref{sec:sim-survey}). In a replay built from actors, a model of
Part~II becomes one more actor that reads the book and sends its predictions to the strategy, and the same code runs
in replay, paper and live trading (Section~\ref{sec:kaspar-actor}).

The second is hardware. Book building takes about 1\,\textmu s from socket to book at the median in \kasparhft{}'s software path and
hundreds of nanoseconds or less in published FPGA designs; published inference times of order-book models range from
about 25\,\textmu s to about 23\,ms, about half of them above one millisecond (Table~\ref{tab:lob-latency}). Section~\ref{sec:hw-why}
identifies four causes---recurrence that must run step by step, attention whose cost grows with the square of the
window, memory traffic at a batch of one, and fixed overheads per operation---and Section~\ref{sec:hw-what} lists the
candidate remedies: shorter or incremental windows, smaller and lower-precision models, fused kernels, and moving the
regular stages to FPGAs. All of these inference times exceed the CME publisher period (the minimum spacing of about
7.5\,\textmu s between packets from CME's market-data publisher), the time within which a stage on the hot path must
handle each packet to avoid queueing~\citep{maciejewski2026packets}. A model too slow for the hot path does not just
answer late. It misses signals and lets the message queue in front of it grow. It also leaves orders to be picked off:
resting orders, and aggressive orders that arrive late and end up resting. Hardware decides how much of a signal's
value survives to the moment of execution.

\subsection{A worked example in \kasparhft{}: one model from forecast to live trading}
\label{sec:worked-kaspar}

The method of this survey can be followed end to end for one model. The example below takes the simplest signal of
Part~II, order-flow imbalance, and carries it from a forecast to live trading in \kasparhft{}. It is a design that uses the
mechanisms described in Part~III, not a reported result.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em,itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Choose what the output is for.} The use is passive execution: buying and selling a fixed quantity in
  each ten-minute window, as in the benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:shadow}. The quantity that matters is therefore at
  levels 4 and 5 of the hierarchy: whether a passive fill will be followed by an adverse move (its mark-out), not
  whether the next price move is up.
\item \textbf{Set the floor.} The two model-free versions, passive \pov{} and \textsc{Shadow-APOV} (aggressive
  percentage-of-volume), are the baselines. On a year of ES data they cost similar amounts, about $+0.095$ ticks per
  contract (Table~\ref{tab:shadow-results}).
  Anything the model adds is measured against both.
\item \textbf{Build the model.} Order-flow imbalance over the last few book events at the best prices
  (Section~\ref{sec:ofi}) is used as a gate. \pov{} places a buy, with a fixed probability, behind an addition by another participant near
  the best bid; with the gate, it does so only when the imbalance does not point to a falling price, and the sell
  symmetrically. The gate has two settings, the window and
  the threshold; they are chosen on a training period, and every setting tried is counted, because the best of many
  settings looks better than it is.
\item \textbf{Add it as an actor.} The model is one more actor (Section~\ref{sec:kaspar-actor}): it subscribes to the
  order book, computes the imbalance after each burst of updates, and sends a prediction message to the strategy
  actor, which checks the gate before it places an order.
\item \textbf{Replay.} The gated strategy runs through the same recorded sessions, with the same target quantity and
  the same delays as the two baselines. Every simulator actor, the model actor included, runs in one group with one
  queue and one thread, so the replay is deterministic (Section~\ref{sec:kaspar-determinism}): run twice, it gives the
  same fills, and any difference from the baselines is due to the gate. Each day runs as a separate process, so a year
  of sessions runs in parallel on one machine.
\item \textbf{Score.} On held-out sessions, compare cost against the arrival mid-price and post-fill mark-outs with
  both baselines, with confidence intervals that treat each session as one independent sample. Check participation too: a gate that declines additions also
  fills less, and the quantity must still be completed. The model is kept only if it beats both versions.
\item \textbf{Check the latency budget.} Order-flow imbalance is a running sum updated by a few additions per
  event, well within the time between packets. A larger model in its place---a deep network reading the same
  book---must be checked against the hot-path budget of Section~\ref{sec:hardware} or taken off the hot path, and the
  replay is repeated with added delays to measure how the gain changes with latency.
\item \textbf{Go to paper and live trading.} The same code runs on live market data with simulated execution (paper
  trading), and then, by changing one configuration setting of the simulated order manager, with orders sent to the
  exchange (Section~\ref{sec:kaspar-equivalence}).
\end{enumerate}
The same steps apply to any model of Part~II: a queue model to skip additions with a low chance of a good fill, a
Hawkes intensity to time participation, a deep forecast to choose between resting and crossing
(Section~\ref{sec:shadow}). Only the third step changes.

\subsection{An order of operations for choosing a model}
\label{sec:order-ops}

For a reader who must choose a model, the survey's guidance reduces to an order of operations.
Decide first what the output is for---aggressive trading, passive execution, market making or
simulation---and therefore which of the targets of Section~\ref{sec:deep} matters. Establish the
strongest simple baseline on the best available representation: order-flow imbalance, queue
imbalance, the micro-price, the queue race (which of the two best queues empties first;
Section~\ref{sec:queue-models}), and for passive orders the volume ahead. Evaluate it with
proper scores and economic metrics in a queue-exact replay, counting every configuration tried. Only
then add capacity---a shallow network, a sequence model, a hybrid---and keep it only if the increment
survives the same test.

Future studies would be easiest to build on if they reported not only whether a model predicts the market,
but what information it extracts, what decision it changes, how that decision is executed, and
whether the incremental value survives the simple-baseline comparison of Section~\ref{sec:tradeability}.
