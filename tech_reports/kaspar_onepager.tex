\vspace{8pt}
\textbf{Kaspar is two things sharing one codebase.} It is a production trading
system --- multicast market data in, full order books reconstructed order by
order, an execution algorithm on top, and authenticated iLink~3 sessions out to
the exchange. It is also a market simulator: the same books rebuilt from recorded
packet captures, with your orders sitting in the price--time queue and filling
only when the market actually trades through them.

The same strategy code, the same execution algorithm
and the same book run in PCAP replay, in live paper trading, and against the live
exchange. You move between them by changing configuration, not by porting
anything --- so a backtest exercises the code path that will trade.

\section{What is in the box}

\vspace{-2pt}
{\color{ink3}\footnotesize No vendor feed handler, no broker API, no assembly required.}
\vspace{4pt}

\noindent\begin{minipage}[t]{0.485\textwidth}
\tile{Market data}{Full \textbf{MDP3} SBE decoder: incremental book updates,
trades, order-by-order MBO, instrument definitions, snapshot recovery. Sequence
gaps are detected and repaired automatically.}
\tile{Order book}{Order-by-order reconstruction holding \textbf{every resting
order individually}, not aggregated depth --- which is what makes a queue
position, and therefore a fill inference, definable at all.}
\tile{Order entry}{\textbf{iLink 3} session handler: SBE encoding, HMAC
authentication, sequence management, negotiation and establishment,
primary/secondary failover.}
\tile{Execution}{\textbf{Shadow-POV}, a passive/aggressive percentage-of-volume algorithm
that places by following orders in the market. Measured over a full calendar
year --- see below.}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.485\textwidth}
\tile{Simulation}{Three modes off one binary: \textbf{PCAP replay}, live
multicast paper trading, live iLink execution. Deterministic --- a fixed session
replays to identical fills.}
\tile{Concurrency}{A custom \textbf{C++20 actor framework} --- a proven
concurrency model, and the same code whether the hot path runs across threads or
entirely on one. Scale out by giving actors their own threads; collapse to a
single-threaded hot path by grouping them, with no rewrite either way.}
\tile{Strategy authoring}{Write strategies as \textbf{C++} in-process actors for
the lowest latency, or in \textbf{Rust} across an in-process FFI interop --- no
socket, no serialisation between the strategy and the book.}
\tile{Measurement}{An execution-cost harness that runs the algorithm as a
two-sided maker over recorded sessions, one row per window, plus a parameter
sweep over rate, size and simulated latency.}
\end{minipage}

\section{Performance}

\vspace{-2pt}
{\color{ink3}\footnotesize AMD EPYC 9374F \quad\textperiodcentered\quad RHEL 9
\quad\textperiodcentered\quad g++ 15 \quad\textperiodcentered\quad
\code{-O3 -march=native}}

The usual objection to actors is messaging overhead. The answer is
\code{fast\_send}, which runs the receiver's handler inline on the caller's
thread and returns the reply as a value.

\section{Shadow-PPOV execution}

\vspace{-2pt}
{\color{ink3}\footnotesize ES futures \quad\textperiodcentered\quad 246 sessions
of calendar 2025 \quad\textperiodcentered\quad 100 contracts bought and 100 sold
per window}
\vspace{4pt}

Most execution algorithms either cross the spread or quote continuously.
Shadow-PPOV takes a third path: on observing a third-party add, it places its own
limit order \textbf{at that order's price, on that order's venue}, records nothing
but the pair of exchange identifiers, and cancels when the order it follows ends.
No order-book model. No fill-probability forecast. No routing computation --- the
venue mix is inherited from the flow rather than solved for.

\vspace{2pt}
{\color{accent}\vrule width 2.5pt}\hspace{5pt}%
\begin{minipage}{0.955\textwidth}
Each window opens with 100 contracts to buy and 100 to sell, worked at once.
\textbf{Relative slippage} prices each leg against the mid at the instant the
window opened and averages the two, so the market's drift during the window
cancels.
\end{minipage}

\vspace{6pt}
The asymmetry is not in what a fill costs. A marketable limit never executes worse
than its limit --- if the book moves in its favour during the flight it simply
fills better. What latency changes is how often it fills at all: when the level it
was priced from has been consumed, the order rests at a price the market has
already left, and the quantity comes back to be re-sent at whatever the price has
become. A resting order has no equivalent failure: a quote that arrives late has
still arrived, and pays at most the width it crossed.

\section{What it does not claim}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em,itemsep=1.5pt,topsep=2pt,label={\color{warm}---}]
  \item \textbf{Adverse selection is not avoided.} The half-spread a resting order
  captures is returned, in expectation, through adverse selection --- which is why
  passive and crossing cost the same here. Post-fill mark-outs show that cost is
  complete within a second of the fill and undiminished at thirty.
  \item \textbf{The replay models no market impact.} Orders fill against the
  recorded feed as though they had not been there. At 4.5\,\% participation that
  assumption is mild; at the 6.9\,\% the passive side reaches under 5\,ms of delay
  it is strained, and the reported participation should be read as an upper bound.
  \item \textbf{Tick-to-trade is a target, not a colocated measurement.}
  \item \textbf{No comparison against a VWAP or TWAP \emph{algorithm} has been
  run.} Beating the market's own volume-weighted price over the interval you
  happened to trade in is a weaker and confounded claim, and the report says so.
  \item \textbf{One instrument, one year.} ES is a one-tick book for most of the
  session; do not carry the results to wider-spread products without re-measuring.
\end{itemize}
