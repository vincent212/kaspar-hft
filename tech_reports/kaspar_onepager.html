Kaspar is two things sharing one codebase. It is a production trading system — multicast market data in, full order books reconstructed order by order, an execution algorithm on top, and authenticated iLink 3 sessions out to the exchange. It is also a market simulator: the same books rebuilt from recorded packet captures, with your orders sitting in the price–time queue and filling only when the market actually trades through them.

The point is that these are not two programs. The same strategy code, the same execution algorithm and the same book run in PCAP replay, in live paper trading, and against the live exchange. You move between them by changing configuration, not by porting anything — so a backtest exercises the code path that will trade.

What is in the box no vendor feed handler, no broker API, no assembly required

Market data

Full MDP3 v12 SBE decoder: incremental book updates, trades, order-by-order MBO, instrument definitions, snapshot recovery. Sequence gaps are detected and repaired automatically.

Order book

Order-by-order reconstruction holding every resting order individually, not aggregated depth — which is what makes a queue position, and therefore a fill inference, definable at all.

Order entry

iLink 3 session handler: SBE encoding, HMAC authentication, sequence management, negotiation and establishment, primary/secondary failover.

Execution

Shadow-PPOV, a passive percentage-of-volume algorithm that places by following individual resting orders. Measured over a full calendar year — see below.

Simulation

Three modes off one binary: PCAP replay, live multicast paper trading, live iLink execution. Deterministic — a fixed session replays to identical fills.

Concurrency

A custom C++20 actor framework. Shared-nothing message passing, O(1) dispatch by integer message id, CPU affinity pinning, pool allocation on the hot path.

Strategy authoring

C++ in-process actors for the lowest latency, or Rust across an in-process FFI interop. A from-scratch Rust port of the actor core ships with a price–time matching engine.

Measurement

An execution-cost harness that runs the algorithm as a two-sided maker over recorded sessions and emits one row per window, plus a parameter sweep over rate, size and simulated latency.

Shadow-PPOV execution ES futures · 246 sessions of calendar 2025

Most execution algorithms either cross the spread or quote continuously. Shadow-PPOV takes a third path: on observing a third-party add, it places its own limit order at that order's price, on that order's venue, records nothing but the pair of exchange identifiers, and cancels when the order it follows ends. No order-book model. No fill-probability forecast. No routing computation — the venue mix is inherited from the flow rather than solved for.

Each window opens with 100 contracts to buy and 100 to sell, worked at once. Relative slippage prices each leg against the mid at the instant the window opened and averages the two, so the market's drift during the window cancels.

What latency costs

The same delay applied to all three paths at once: the outbound order, the outbound cancel, and the inbound market-data feed.

The asymmetry is not in what a fill costs. A marketable limit never executes worse than its limit — if the book moves in its favour during the flight it simply fills better. What latency changes is how often it fills at all: when the level it was priced from has been consumed, the order rests at a price the market has already left, and the quantity comes back to be re-sent at whatever the price has become. A resting order has no equivalent failure. A quote that arrives late has still arrived, and pays at most the width it crossed.

What it does not claim

  • Adverse selection is not avoided. The half-spread a resting order captures is returned, in expectation, through adverse selection — which is why passive and crossing cost the same here. Post-fill mark-outs show that cost is complete within a second of the fill and undiminished at thirty.
  • The replay models no market impact. Orders fill against the recorded feed as though they had not been there. At 4.5 % participation that assumption is mild; at the 6.9 % the passive side reaches under 5 ms of delay it is strained, and the reported participation should be read as an upper bound.
  • Tick-to-trade is a target, not a colocated measurement. It is derived from component benchmarks and labelled expected wherever it appears.
  • No comparison against a VWAP or TWAP algorithm has been run. Beating the market's own volume-weighted price over the interval you happened to trade in is a weaker and confounded claim, and the report says so.
  • One instrument, one year. ES is a one-tick book for most of the session; the results should not be carried to wider-spread products without re-measurement.
